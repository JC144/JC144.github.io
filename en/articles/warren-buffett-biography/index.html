Reading The Snowball Effect

Notes on Warren Buffett’s biography, from his earliest investments to how he chose and ran businesses.

It took me just over a year to read Warren Buffett’s biography. It covers eighty years in the life of a very active man who met a great many people. The book goes into detail about the businesses Buffett bought and the people he met and worked with along the way. Above all, it gives you a better sense of the man himself and of how skewed our understanding of his famous snowball effect can be.

Compound interest

People often talk about Warren Buffett and his legendary snowball. The usual example goes like this. You invest 100 coins every month. Your investment earns interest, which gets added to the amount you invested, and little by little your snowball gets bigger. One of his other sayings gets less attention: trees don’t grow to the sky.

Warren Buffett worked hard. He was frugal. He was also brilliant. As a boy, he worked as a messenger and reinvested his earnings. As a teenager, he bought a farm that brought him an income. He tried his hand at the stock market early on, too. He managed to study under his idol and went on to learn by working alongside him. That’s where he developed his philosophy.

Cigar butts

Warren didn’t just buy at regular intervals. He bought when a business was genuinely undervalued. Ideally, its stock market value would be close to its book value. If he could sell off everything the company owned and break even, it was worth a look. Then he’d look at its potential. He wasn’t out to liquidate businesses, but he wasn’t a gambler either. Most of the time, he made deals he couldn’t lose. He soon started aiming for a controlling interest in the companies he invested in. A handful of shares wouldn’t do. He wanted a majority stake so he could keep the people in charge or put good managers in place. One thing I love about the later part of the book is how completely he immerses himself in a business, trying to understand where there’s room for improvement. That outside perspective is something I really value when it comes to innovation.

Another important point is that once Warren Buffett buys something, it might as well be locked in a vault. He very rarely sells! (HODL!) So how do you keep investing without money coming in? Borrow it? Warren Buffett is against borrowing. It’s a risk, and he doesn’t like risk.

Instead, he built businesses that generated cash he could reinvest. His insurance groups are a good example. Here’s what I took away from his life story:

  1. Buy undervalued businesses.
  2. Get them back on their feet by sorting out the management, cutting costs, paying down debt…
  3. Build cash-generating businesses so you can invest more.
  4. Repeat.
  5. Profit! Or maybe not, since his main aim was to grow the capital of the group.

Trees don’t grow to the sky, but they can send up plenty of little shoots!

Beyond all that, I found the man himself fascinating. A bit of a loner, with a big heart and a deep sense of honour. Humble, with no taste for luxury. “He won’t eat anything a three-year-old wouldn’t touch.”
