The Innovator’s Dilemma

Clayton Christensen uses a series of examples to show how an innovation can bring down the giants that dominate a market.

It took a while, but I’ve finally finished The Innovator’s Dilemma by Clayton M. Christensen.

The book itself is fairly tough going. It reads like a thesis, with a scientific approach, lots of sources and plenty of graphs.

What it has to say, though, really helped me understand how innovation happens and, in particular, why big companies find it so hard to innovate.

A serial entrepreneur strongly recommended it to me when I wanted to understand what makes a project disruptive and why big companies don’t necessarily see the technology that’ll knock them off the top coming.

Here’s my attempt at a summary of the main points.

The examples

Throughout the book, the author illustrates his argument with examples of large companies and whole industries that have been transformed.

  • Kodak, the inventor of the Polaroid and the leader in photographic film, gradually lost its position to digital photography.
  • In the hard drive industry, drives kept getting smaller while their performance improved.
  • HP and its printers made the transition from laser to inkjet.
  • Cable-operated excavators gave way to hydraulic excavators.
  • Electric cars are another example. The shift from petrol to electric has all the ingredients of disruption :)

What they have in common

Christensen then looks at what these companies and the emerging innovations have in common.

A technology that performs worse

The first thing that stands out is that the emerging technology often performs worse than the established one. The new hard drives were physically smaller, but they also held less data. Early digital pictures looked laughable. Hydraulic excavators were underpowered.

A different market

With a technology that performs worse, it’s hard to compete head-on in the same market. These products found specific niches. Think of hard drives for home PCs or embedded systems, excavators for digging house foundations rather than mines, or printers for homes instead of offices.

A market with little appeal for the incumbent

In the early days of personal computing, home PCs accounted for a negligible share of the market as far as a large server company was concerned. The margins on digging house foundations were too slim to justify using a cable excavator.

For a newcomer, though, the market is appealing.

Put it this way. A €1 million market represents just 0.1% of the turnover of a company doing €1 billion in business. There’s little incentive for that company to enter it.

For a newcomer, €1 million is a sizeable market.

A technology that improves exponentially

Hard drives and digital cameras benefited from a version of Moore’s law, the idea that computing power doubles every eighteen months. Quality and performance improved exponentially. Smaller hard drives soon caught up with conventional models. Digital photos first matched the quality of film and then surpassed it. Once a niche market had paid for the product’s development, it could break into a much broader market.
It reminds me of Peter Thiel’s idea of building a monopoly of your own in Zero to One.

The incumbent tries to catch up

The big company sees its market share shrinking and takes notice of the competition. It has a few options:

  • Copy the competitor by trying to put the same processes in place.
  • Acquire the company and its technology.
  • Keep doing what it’s been doing.

Unfortunately, that isn’t always enough. Plenty of companies have disappeared after a takeover. We’ve also seen successes that others copied but struggled to match (hello, Samsung, Apple and Microsoft).

Why?

Listening to users

When Steve Ballmer was in charge of Microsoft, he often said things along the lines of “Nobody’s going to buy that technology.” He listened too closely to his users and what they needed. There’s a line attributed to Ford: “If I’d listened to my users, they’d have wanted faster horses.” And they’d have missed out on the car. A disruptive technology meets needs that users may not yet have noticed themselves.

The existing user base

It’s hard to change your users’ habits. People often resist change fiercely, even if they eventually make the switch of their own accord. A company known for one particular product will struggle to sell a more innovative one that works a little differently. It isn’t what its users expect of it.

Confusing processes and resources

A company’s resources are its people and its technology.
Its processes have more to do with its culture and know-how.
Acquiring one and overlooking the other is often why a takeover fails. Plenty of businesses have been bought by large groups and gone on to struggle to sustain their success. Either the resources walked out the door when the best people left, or the acquiring company swallowed up the culture.

In that respect, I really like Facebook’s approach. It buys a company, gives it more resources and then gives it space to carry on with its own culture and brand, as it did with Instagram and WhatsApp. Google has an interesting approach too, giving each business a degree of independence under Alphabet.

In conclusion

When someone asks you “What’s stopping Google from doing the same thing?”, here are a few answers:

  • “The market is too small for them.” (Know your numbers!)
  • “We’ve spent the past X years immersed in this. We’re the people best placed to do it. In two or three years, they might well be interested in us.”
  • “We can become the leader in this particular market very quickly. Then our plan is to expand here, and then there.”

Choose a market that fits your product, pinpoint the feature that gives it disruptive potential and go for it!
