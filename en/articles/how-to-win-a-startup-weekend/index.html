How to Win a Startup Weekend

From Friday’s opening pitch to Sunday’s presentation to the judges, here’s how to make the most of the 54 hours of a Startup Weekend.

A Startup Weekend gives you 54 hours to turn an idea into a startup. You can find more information on the Toulouse Startup Weekend page.

It’s a great event for working on an idea you care about. Of course, you can also come along and lend your particular skills to someone else’s project.
I’ve written this mainly for people bringing an idea of their own.

The idea

Finding an idea deserves an article of its own. Let’s assume you already have something in mind. Come prepared!

Look at the competition, find out who else is involved and think about how you might make money. Know your subject and people will take you more seriously.

Know why you want to do this, too. Simon Sinek’s “Start with Why” means a lot to me. If you can put into words what drives you to bring a project to life, you can give other people a reason to join you.

Oh, and give your project a name before you show up!

Friday night

Winning over the room

You have one minute to present your idea. Your job is to win over the room. Everyone will vote for their three favourite ideas. Yours needs to make that top three.
There’ll be around sixty pitches. You have to stand out.

A few things that help:

  • Give your project a name!
  • Give it a memorable name! Put it on a sign big enough to read from across the room. A sheet of A4 paper turned sideways is the bare minimum.
  • Wear your project’s colours. Be its mascot.
  • If you have something to show, show it. A minute is short, but it’s enough to show you haven’t turned up empty-handed. Travel project? Bring a backpack. Wine project? Set up a tasting stand. Sports project? Grab a wig…

Then there’s the pitch itself. A minute is both short and long (yes, really…). Get to the point. You want to see people’s eyes light up, and you want to make the most of the time you have.

  • Why are you doing this? What problem do you want to solve? What’s missing that you could provide?
  • How will you do it? What sets you apart? What’s the clever bit?
  • The name of the project! The name of the project!
  • If you have time, say who you need and what they can help with. It shows there’s still work to do and explains why you’re here in the first place.

For example, my pitch for Deewile went something like this:

I’ve always loved looking at the stars. But it’s been a long time since I’ve seen them. Life gets in the way, the city lights get in the way, there’s never enough time… Dreams are a bit like that. As kids we have so many. As we get older we forget them, until one day we wish we’d followed through. With DEEWILE, we want to change that! We want to help you rediscover your dreams, be there as you make them happen and help you tell your story so someone else starts dreaming too.

So if you’d like to dream with us, we’re looking for inspired designers and ambitious marketers ready to sell the dream! :)

Don’t bother introducing yourself. Few people will remember your name. The project’s name matters more (and with a first name like mine, you’re already fifteen seconds down).

Canvassing for votes

Everyone’s pitched. Now you need votes. Each person has three votes to cast and can only give one to any single project.

It’s time to become a walking billboard. Grab your sign and go sell your project to the other participants to get as many votes as you can. Ideally, bring along a few enthusiastic people you know who can spread the word and pick up more votes quickly.

Those votes go fast. Get moving!

Putting your team together

Your project made the cut! Friday night isn’t over yet. You still have a team to put together.

A few pointers:

  • Aim for five to seven people, tops. A bigger team gets harder to manage.
  • Know who you’re looking for. Think about what they can bring to the project and what the project can offer them.
  • Recruit for the skills you need. It’s fine to turn someone down or point them towards another project.

Briefing

Things are finally settling down.

Find a room where you can all sit down for a bit. I usually start by going around the table. Everyone shares their name, what they do and something they’re passionate about. We talk, have a laugh, let off a little steam and get ready for a great weekend.
This is also your chance to go through the project in more detail, show the team what resources you already have and explain what you’re hoping they’ll do over the weekend.

Get some rest, soldier

It’s probably around 11 pm by now. Go get some rest. There’s little point in starting something new at this hour.

Saturday

Brainstorming

Saturday morning is a chance to get to know each other while you brainstorm the different parts of the project you could develop.

New ideas and different directions will come up.

Splitting up to do research

Depending on the size of the team, it can help to break into smaller groups to test assumptions, look things up, make phone calls and so on.

Comparing notes

Early in the afternoon, compare what you’ve found. New hypotheses will emerge. Dig a little deeper, check which ideas could actually work and figure out how you could make money from them.

Arguments, doubts and a break

Towards the end of the day, people get tired and tempers can flare. You start thinking you’ll never finish in time. Go get a snack or have dinner. There’ll be a few talks in the evening. Go along, learn something and clear your head.

Before you call it a night, put your ideas in some sort of order. Let your mind sort through them while you sleep.

Sunday

Starting again from scratch

Things often look a lot clearer on Sunday. Come back to the original idea with the benefit of everything you discovered on Saturday. Once the pieces fit together, start working on the presentation around mid-morning.

Getting the slides and the pitch ready

This is the outline I generally use for my slides and pitches:

  1. The problem in broad terms
  2. The specific problem we’re tackling
  3. “That’s why we created…” Introduce the product.
  4. How it works
  5. How we’ll make money
  6. The fantastic team that’ll make it happen
  7. Our plan for world domination
  8. A final slide with a killer line (“Small farmers are the answer”, “Our dream is to make yours come true”, “At Drawall, we’re already drawing up plans”)

A good pitch is one you know inside out without learning it by heart. A bit of a paradox :)

You have four minutes. It’s intense. You need to remember everything and stick to time. That means rehearsing it, rehearsing it and rehearsing it again. Try your lines on the team. Do the transitions work? Do people understand the pitch? Try it out on your coaches, too. You want it to feel like second nature.

The project lead doesn’t have to give the pitch, although I’d recommend it. Give the audience one speaker to follow, two at most. Any more and you’ll lose them.

Pierre Morsa has written a piece with plenty of pointers for getting your message across.

The grand finale — your pitch!

This is it. You’re nearly up. Here come the nerves!

Take slow, deep breaths, loosen up, jump up and down a little (yes, I like to jump around before a pitch…).

You know what you want to say. You’ll be fine. Just remember to keep the mic in front of your mouth and go easy on the hand gestures ;-)

You get four minutes to pitch, then four minutes for questions. If you’ve done your homework, you know your subject better than the judges do. Get your team up on stage and direct each question to the right person. Keep the answers brief so the judges can ask everything they want to. You don’t want them walking away with unanswered questions.

If you don’t know the answer, be honest. Tell them they’ve raised an interesting point and you’ll need to check. Judges have a built-in bullshit detector. Don’t try to bluff them!

Keep an eye on who’s taking questions. There’s always someone on a team who’ll jump in just to say something, even when they don’t know the answer. Be firm, but respectful!

The winners

You can breathe again. The hard work is over and the judges are deliberating. Go and chat with the other participants over something to eat.

The judges’ decision doesn’t matter much, honestly. You’ve worked on your project, met a team and swapped business cards. You can go and build your startup.

The winner may get a bit more support, but you can still launch your project.

That’s what makes Startup Weekend special. Every year several startups come out of the event. Wherever you finish, the people you meet and the contacts you make are what really count :)

You’ve already won!

The Startup Weekend blues

After such an intense weekend, give it a week for the excitement to settle, then meet up in a bar to take stock. Who wants to carry on? Who wants to stop? How are you going to organise things?
As the person who brought the idea, you don’t have to keep working with everyone. Focus on the people whose skills will help you move the project forward.

Then go for it!
