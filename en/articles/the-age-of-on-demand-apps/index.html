The Age of On-Demand Apps

Is the era of off-the-shelf software over?

Every week I come across someone who’s “automated their house hunt.” They scrape the big property sites, plug in their own search criteria and filter the listings.

The software engineer in me sees time wasted reinventing the wheel and feels for the poor sites that are going to get scraped more and more. Once I get past that frustration, though, I see a trend that interests me. We’re entering the age of on-demand apps.

Since the early days of computing, common problems have been tackled by a handful of researchers, companies or open-source communities. Ideally, they’d come together around a single project and establish a standard (though we’ve always been tempted to make the same thing all over again, just a little differently).

As users, that left us with a small number of options. Think of it as ready-to-wear software, or what we usually call “off the shelf.”

That comes with its frustrations.

  • How do you tackle a task that spans several tools? Who hasn’t had to help a relative “just” copy their photos into the Pictures folder?
  • Then there’s enshittification, as apps pick up features nobody asked for. Who hasn’t groaned at a “rate this app” prompt popping up halfway through a task?
  • Sometimes the way you use an app just doesn’t match its default behaviour. Take having to switch to the Following feed every time you open Instagram.

AI is making it easier to build software tailored to our needs and to adapt the tools we already use.

For now, that’s mostly the preserve of a few power users. But it’ll become more widely accessible and, more importantly, make computing easier for people who don’t know their way around it.

Claude and ChatGPT are already starting to bring rich, custom interfaces right into their chats. That’s one less app to open.

The assistant will take on more and more until it becomes an operating system in its own right. Little by little, it’ll connect to all the services you need to write a document, show a presentation, send an email or manage a calendar, generating interfaces along the way in the universal language of the web.

There will still be times when we need an app that relies on a specific context, probably one we share with others. Those apps can use that same intelligence to adapt to the person using them, changing the wording, trimming down the menus and adjusting the interface.

The services themselves will have work to do, too. To go back to my opening example, a property site becomes a data source. Fighting scraping will just drive up costs. It would make more sense to make the listings easier to access and provide a point of contact to verify the lead.

As for where the value in software will come from, I think it’ll shift to brand, expertise and trust. I’ll come back to that in another post.
